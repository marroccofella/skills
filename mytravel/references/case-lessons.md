# Case lessons

Generalised lessons from a historical travel review. Exact dates, prices, routes, account facts and source transcripts remain in the private originating record. These are not current fares or rules.

## A short paid link can unlock a useful network

The assistant treated a major city as compulsory, then compared only direct taxis from coach stops to the final destination. It recommended an intermediate shopping-area stop partly on a claimed short café walk. The traveller found a much longer walk and a live taxi quote well above the generic estimate. The assistant then suggested returning through the major city.

The traveller proposed a short taxi to a nearby railway station with useful onward trains. This expanded a choice between long taxi and all-public-transport into a mixed-mode journey.

Failure: premature hub anchoring, comparing only direct final-leg taxis, conflating similarly named locations, and instructions before checking walking distance, actual fare and timed onward travel.

Rule: test short links into useful networks and verify the chain before advising an en-route exit. Shortest distance generates candidates; it does not prove lowest total price or earliest arrival. The record did not establish final paid taxi/train fares or actual arrival, so the improved candidate cannot be called a proven global optimum.

## Existing account value can fund a complete connecting journey

An authorised email search exposed a dated airline-mile balance, and the user recovered account access. Repeated advice focused on an unverified long-haul Business award and buying the feeder separately. The user's eventual confirmation showed a single partner award covering the entire connecting journey, with low cash outlay and Economy on both sectors.

Success: discovering existing value and redeeming it for the required routing. Limit: this was not a Business redemption or proof of a later upgrade. The email snapshot alone did not establish a current usable balance.

Rule: discover relevant assets early, query full origin/destination awards as well as components, and compare acceptable cabin trade-offs. Access/tool failure is not absent inventory. Prevent stranded purchases without making separate tickets the default.

## The intended onward journey can improve price and benefits

The user proposed one ticket through a desired week-long stopover to the eventual destination. The supplier's final-review comparison priced both Business flights slightly below the identical first flight alone. Several arrival airports tied; the selected one suited the overnight preference and ground journey. Later eligibility checks found useful complimentary airport transfers.

Rule: test through pricing, allowed dates, cabins and final-address access together. Confirm usable benefits before ranking and then reserve them separately when required. Included transfers remained unsubmitted at the last observation because operational details were missing.

Do not describe average cost per segment as a lower payable total, repeat an unsupported remembered saving, or claim superiority over every seller from a bounded comparison. Cheaper agency and mixed-cabin leads still needed complete validation. A shared fare-family name did not imply identical change/refund conditions.

## Generalisation boundaries

The lesson is systematic variation plus exact verification. It is not "always use miles", "always use a stopover", "always get off early", "always buy a return", or "always choose the latest pickup". Recover current intent each time; evidence and a usable fallback matter more than persuasive description.
