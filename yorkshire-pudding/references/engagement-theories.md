# Ten attention and recall lenses

These are evidence-informed lenses for making public writing more memorable,
not a recipe for guaranteed reach. Apply one or two that fit the message,
audience, and format. Do not stack every trigger into one piece.

## 1. High-arousal transmission

High-arousal feelings such as awe, delight, surprise, anger, or anxiety can
increase sharing; low-arousal sadness tends to spread less. Prefer truthful
awe, useful surprise, humour, or constructive urgency. Do not manufacture fear,
rage, or panic merely to win reach.

## 2. Social value

People share things that help them express identity, taste, competence, or
belonging. Give the reader a genuinely useful insight or an unexpectedly clear
way to explain something. Let the reader look helpful, not superior.

## 3. Information-gap curiosity

Curiosity grows when a reader can see a specific gap between what they know and
what they want to know. Name the gap, make the payoff honest, and resolve it in
the post or thread. Never use a misleading cliffhanger or hide the answer to
force a click.

## 4. Narrative transportation

Stories can hold attention through concrete scenes, people, stakes, change, and
mental imagery. Start close to the moment, show what changed, and land on the
meaning. Use only real experience, attributed examples, or clearly labelled
fiction; never invent a personal story for the author.

## 5. Processing fluency

Readable, concrete language is easier to process and can support likes, shares,
and comments. Use one main idea, familiar words, short paragraphs, active verbs,
and purposeful cadence. Clarity does not mean flattening a distinctive voice.

## 6. Self-reference and identity resonance

People notice messages that connect to their goals, roles, communities, or
values. Say who the point is for and why it matters to them. Invite recognition
without assigning an identity, stereotyping a group, or pretending to know the
reader's private feelings.

## 7. Evidence and descriptive norms

Real evidence can reduce uncertainty: a named source, transparent sample, date,
or clearly labelled customer quote. Report the limits as well as the result.
Never fabricate followers, comments, testimonials, consensus, or “everyone is
doing this” language.

## 8. Reciprocity and participation

Useful contributions can create a healthy norm of people helping one another.
Give a practical answer, example, template, or generous acknowledgement before
asking for a reply. Ask a real question when another person's view is wanted;
never guilt people into commenting or sharing.

## 9. Novelty and distinctiveness

A fresh contrast, uncommon detail, or clear point of view helps a post stand
out. Earn the difference with evidence or lived specificity. Do not perform
contrarianism, invent a hot take, or distort a fact just to appear original.

## 10. Peak-end and return value

People often remember a strong moment and the ending. Give the post one clear
turn, image, line, or useful conclusion, then offer a legitimate next step. A
series may create anticipation, but never withhold essential information or
promise a payoff the next post cannot deliver.

## Natural-writing check

Before publishing, check that the draft has:

- one specific detail a generic writer would not choose;
- a real point of view, with uncertainty preserved where it belongs;
- varied sentence length and a natural spoken rhythm;
- a concrete action, answer, or question rather than a vague engagement prompt;
- no invented first-person experience, fake imperfection, or copied “viral”
  formula.

Remove generic openings, inflated adjectives, repetitive headings, empty
“agree?” questions, ornamental jargon, and identical calls to action. Read it
aloud. If the draft could be pasted unchanged into a hundred contexts, add
truth and particularity before adding more persuasion.

## Evidence notes

The strongest directly relevant evidence here concerns emotional arousal and
sharing, readability, curiosity, narrative transportation, and reciprocity.
The remaining lenses are useful transfers from communication and
social-psychology research; treat them as hypotheses to test against observed
reader response, not laws of reach.
