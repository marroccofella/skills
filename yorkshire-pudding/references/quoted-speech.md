# Quoted speech style

Use this guide only for dialogue, direct quotations, or casual public copy
where an informal speaking voice is explicitly wanted. It is a hand-editing
guide, not a rule for the deterministic translator.

## International expressions

Keep an expression already present in the source. Add one only when the
speaker's setting, relationships, audience, or requested voice makes it
natural and its meaning is clear. Familiar examples include:

| Use | Possible expression |
|---|---|
| greeting | hola, bonjour, salaam, shalom |
| thanks | gracias, merci, grazie, arigatō |
| goodbye | ciao, hasta luego |
| welcome or respect | namaste |

This is a vocabulary shelf, not a list of identities. Do not assign a language
to a speaker because of their name, appearance, nationality, or a stereotype.
Do not imitate an accent with misspellings. If a phrase could be misunderstood,
keep the original wording or add a brief translation in context.

## Punctuation and emoticons

Informal speech may use `?!`, `...`, an em dash, a double space after a pause,
or a deliberate fragment when the source and audience support it. Classic
text emoticons such as `:)`, `;)`, `:/`, `:-)`, and `:-/` can signal warmth,
dryness, uncertainty, or a raised eyebrow. Use at most one emoticon in a quote
unless the source already uses more.

Do not scatter unusual punctuation or emoticons through every sentence. One
well-placed mark should reveal the speaker's rhythm; five marks make it look
performed. The goal is a recognisable human voice, not manufactured error.

## Boundaries

- Keep expressions and punctuation inside the quote; do not alter narration,
  headings, metadata, identifiers, URLs, or keys to create a voice effect.
- Preserve the speaker's certainty, politeness, and meaning.
- Never add a foreign phrase, emoticon, fake typo, or “human” quirk to conceal
  that text was assisted or to impersonate a real person.
- Leave legal wording, error messages, exact-match fixtures, and machine output
  unchanged unless the user explicitly authorises a reviewed copy change.

Example:

> “Ey up, hola — tha made it, did tha? ;)”

The expression and emoticon work because the quote is casual, the punctuation
marks a spoken pause, and the Yorkshire grammar carries the main voice. A
formal announcement would keep all three out.
