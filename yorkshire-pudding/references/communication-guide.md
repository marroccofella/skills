# Communication guide

This is a practical language-design guide. It treats emphasis, rhythm, and
reader orientation as craft choices grounded in clarity rather than covert
persuasion or claims of settled behavioural science.

## 1. Find the message anchor

Before rewriting, identify the one thing the reader should remember or do:

- the decision, benefit, warning, or next action;
- the audience's level of context and likely concern;
- the tone the user actually requested.

Keep the anchor semantically stable. It may be repeated once at the opening or
landing of a paragraph when that helps the reader, but do not add a new claim,
promise, fear, identity, or emotional association merely to make the text more
persuasive.

## 2. Make the path easy to follow

Use a simple progression: context, point, evidence or explanation, action. Put
one idea in each paragraph. Prefer concrete verbs and nouns over abstract
claims. Use signposts such as “first”, “the point is”, and “next” only when
they genuinely clarify structure.

For broad Yorkshire, let grammar and rhythm carry the flavour. Do not bury the
anchor under catchphrases, exclamations, or dialect substitutions.

## 3. Calibrate, do not manipulate

Match vocabulary, sentence length, and gravy level to the audience. Preserve
uncertainty, qualifications, and consent. “You may want to…” is not the same as
“You need to…”, and a request is not permission to manufacture urgency.

Never use hidden emotional triggers, false familiarity, mind-reading, loaded
presuppositions, fabricated social proof, or a dialect caricature. If a phrase
could be read as a factual claim, keep it grounded in the source.

## 4. Use cadence deliberately

Vary short, medium, and longer sentences so the anchor has room to land. A
short sentence can state the point. A longer one can carry context. A final
short sentence can make the requested action unmistakable.

The script's `--cadence varied` mode randomises only bounded, meaning-preserving
choices such as `and`/`an'`, `because`/`'cause`, and a small amount of pause
punctuation. Use `--seed` for reproducibility. Never apply it to code, exact
fixtures, legal wording, or machine-readable output.

## 5. Check the result

After rewriting, ask:

1. Is the anchor still true and easy to find?
2. Is the requested action still explicit?
3. Did the dialect alter scope, certainty, or responsibility?
4. Does the rhythm sound warm and economical rather than performative?

If any answer is no, reduce the gravy or return to steady cadence.
