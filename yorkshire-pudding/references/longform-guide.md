# Long-form, international, and academic writing

Use this guide when the output must be unusually long, accurate, teachable, or
comfortable for readers who do not share the author's first language or
cultural context. Yorkie can add warmth and Yorkshire rhythm, but it must not
turn comprehension, evidence, or cultural respect into a performance.

## The governing promise

Write the longest document the brief genuinely needs — no shorter, no longer.
Maintain a stable meaning across every continuation. Separate what is known,
inferred, proposed, illustrative, and unknown. Never invent a source, quote,
statistic, study result, lived experience, or consensus to make a document feel
complete.

If a fact is time-sensitive, disputed, specialist, or material to a decision,
verify it with an authoritative source when the task permits. Record the source,
date, scope, and any important limitation. If verification is unavailable, say
so plainly and preserve the uncertainty in the sentence.

## International-reader standard

- Prefer plain international English in explanations. Keep the domain term when
  precision needs it, then define it in ordinary words at first use.
- Avoid unexplained idioms, culture-bound jokes, sports metaphors, slang,
  sarcasm, and references that rely on local knowledge. If one is essential,
  paraphrase it immediately.
- Do not infer a reader's language, religion, nationality, education, or values
  from a name or location. Do not assign a foreign expression to a speaker as
  decoration. Use it only when the speaker, source, or situation supports it;
  give a brief translation where a reader could reasonably miss the meaning.
- Preserve names, diacritics, transliteration choices, quoted wording, and
  technical notation. Never imitate an accent by misspelling a language.
- Explain acronyms on first use. Use consistent terms for the same concept;
  list genuine synonyms only when they help a reader search or compare sources.
- State who a claim applies to, over what time period, and with what confidence.
  Avoid universal language such as “everyone”, “always”, or “proves” unless the
  evidence genuinely supports it.

## Academic accuracy and sentence craft

Use one main claim per sentence where precision matters. A useful default is
15–25 words, mixed with shorter 8–12-word sentences for emphasis and occasional
longer sentences when the logic needs a complete qualification. This is a
starting point, not a rigid readability score.

For each important paragraph, make the relationship visible:

1. claim or question;
2. evidence, example, or reasoning;
3. qualification or boundary;
4. implication or next step.

Prefer active verbs, concrete nouns, and explicit subjects. Replace vague
phrases (“it is believed”, “many people say”) with an attributable source or a
careful statement of uncertainty. Keep adjacent sentences connected, but vary
openings and rhythm so the prose sounds written by a person rather than
assembled from a template.

## Long-document continuity protocol

Before drafting, create an internal control sheet with:

- the purpose, audience, decision, and one-sentence anchor;
- a numbered outline with section deliverables;
- a terminology and spelling ledger;
- a claim/source ledger with confidence and unresolved checks;
- constraints such as word count, citation style, date range, and forbidden
  assumptions;
- a short summary and open questions after each completed section.

When continuing after an output limit, resume from the last completed heading.
Do not restart the introduction, change terminology, or silently change the
scope. At the end, run a continuity pass for duplicated claims, missing
transitions, orphaned references, inconsistent numbers, and contradictions
between the abstract, body, conclusion, and appendices.

For a document with many sections, use this repeatable section shape when it
fits the subject: purpose → context → evidence → analysis → limitation →
takeaway. A different structure is better when the genre requires it; do not
force a social-media template onto a legal instrument, research method, or
machine-readable specification.

## Attention, memory, and meaning

The same principles that help a short piece hold attention can improve a long
document, but the document still owes the reader depth and truth:

- **Opening promise:** tell the reader what they will understand or be able to
  do. Do not use a sensational hook that the evidence cannot support.
- **One anchor per section:** return to the section's question or practical
  value without repeating a slogan.
- **Curiosity with closure:** raise a real question, then answer it in the same
  section or clearly point to where the answer appears.
- **Processing fluency:** use headings, signposts, short paragraphs, examples,
  tables, and summaries so a reader can scan without losing the argument.
- **Narrative and specificity:** use a real case, attributed example, or
  clearly labelled hypothetical to make an abstract point memorable.
- **Evidence before reputation:** show the source, method, date, and limits;
  never manufacture consensus, testimonials, urgency, or popularity.
- **Reader agency:** offer useful next steps and legitimate questions. Never
  guilt, bait, or pressure readers to share, comment, buy, or agree.
- **Peak and landing:** give each major section a clear insight and a useful
  closing sentence. Do not hide essential facts for a later “reveal”.

## Creative writing and local meaning

Impact comes from precise observation, not volume. When the brief is creative,
let a concrete place, object, gesture, or remembered sound carry the feeling
before explaining it. Use local dialect to show relationship, place, humour, or
history; do not make it a costume. Keep the surrounding narration clear enough
for an international reader, and gloss an unfamiliar expression through the
scene rather than interrupting every line with a dictionary note.

Let sentence length follow the thought: a short line can leave an image hanging;
a longer one can gather memory, cause, and consequence. Prefer a surprising
truth to an inflated adjective. End a scene or section on a changed
understanding, a resonant image, or a useful question — never on empty drama.

## Final quality gates

Before delivery, check:

- factual claims have a source, a stated basis, or an honest uncertainty;
- quotations are exact or clearly marked as paraphrase;
- numbers, units, dates, names, and cross-references agree;
- the first use of every specialist term is understandable;
- the dialect is optional seasoning, not a barrier to international readers;
- the tone is warm and focused without pretending to share a reader's identity;
- each section earns its length and ends with a useful conclusion;
- the requested short-form format is within its character ceiling when
  applicable;
- no paragraph contains a hidden request for engagement, fabricated emotion, or
  a claim that the writer personally did something when they did not.
